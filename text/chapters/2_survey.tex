\chapter{Music Consumption Survey}\label{ch:survey}

To support the choice of topic and show that a music streaming platform
focused on social interactions could be useful, a short survey was conducted.
It looked at personal music listening and discovery habits, how people use
platforms, how people interact about music, and opinions
on possible social features on the platform.


\section{Methods}
The service chosen for the questionnaire was Google Forms~\parencite{googleforms}.
It has a simple interface for creating surveys, makes the form easy to share,
and can export results as CSV files.

The questionnaire consists of 15 questions.
Most are multiple-choice with fixed options, and some allow a custom answer.
All custom answers are grouped under the ``other'' category in the figures below.
Answers in their original form can be found in the full survey.

The complete questionnaire is included in the thesis repository under
\path{text/extras/survey-questions.pdf} and online.\footnote{\url{https://docs.google.com/forms/d/1vhhAu_SfuHV4xy6JoDaRsM5uCElYn_csTmN8u4ZlpHc}}


\section{Results}
This section presents the survey results and discusses what they mean.
Not all questions are discussed. For example, some questions about music discovery are left out.

A total of 119 people took part, with most being in the 18 to 30 age group,
with the exact distribution shown in \path{text/charts/age.png}.

\subsection{Engagement}
First, the survey needed to find out whether respondents listen
to music often and whether they use streaming platforms for it.

Over 50\% of respondents reported listening to more than 500 songs per month,
i.e., 16 songs a day on average
(\path{text/charts/song amount month.png}).
Nearly 80\% stated that they listen to music daily
(\path{text/charts/listening regularity.png}).

The numbers show that people are very interested in music, and
the next step was to find out how they mostly access it.

\subsection{Listening Methods}
As shown in \path{text/charts/constumption_method.png}, the share of respondents using streaming
platforms across all age groups is close to 100\%, with slightly higher
numbers in the younger age groups.
Although downloaded files and physical media are still used,
especially among people aged 30 to 60, they are usually only a secondary option next to streaming services.

The question about the popularity of individual platforms showed that Spotify is the most used one,
which matches global statistics~\parencite{spotifypopularity}.
However, Yandex Music, coming in second place, needs further explanation.
Most of the respondents were from Russian-speaking countries, specifically Russia, and,
with many Western companies leaving the Russian market in 2022, including music streaming services~\parencite{spotify_russia_exit},
most users have switched to local products: Yandex Music~\parencite{yandex_music}, VK Music~\parencite{vk_music}, and
Zvuk~\parencite{zvuk}.
This also explains why other popular region-specific platforms,
such as Amazon Music~(US), QQ Music~(China),
and JioSaavn~(India), do not appear in the survey.

See \path{text/charts/streaming platform.png} for the complete statistics.

The survey showed that streaming services are indeed widely used.
The next step was to look at how often music-related social interactions happen.

\subsection{Social Interactions}
The results of Question 10 show that nearly all respondents (99.2\%) said they listen to music with
others at least sometimes, mostly in offline settings such as gatherings
or car journeys (\path{text/charts/listen together method.png}).
Online shared listening methods, although less common, were also mentioned by a quarter
of respondents.

Question 11 (\path{text/charts/listen together freq.png}) looks at how often these interactions happen:
the results show that many respondents take part in shared listening regularly.
Around 60\% said they do so at least a few times per month,
with a smaller group reporting almost daily interactions.
This supports the earlier idea in \chapterref{ch:intro}
that listening to music is a common social activity.

The next question asks: ``How often do you/your friends share/discuss music?''.
While the previous question focused on real-time in-person shared listening,
this one looks at more deliberate and active music-related interactions, such as sharing songs,
recommending music, or discussing it.
Here, music is the main point of the activity, not background.
The same trend as before appears in the responses:
over 70\% (\path{text/charts/share freq.png}) said that they share music at least several
times a month, with many doing so weekly or more often.
This further confirms that music plays an active social role.

Question 13, ``How does that happen?'' (in terms of music sharing),
asks about the specific ways people share music.
Many respondents (\path{text/charts/share method.png}) said that they usually send links
from streaming platforms through external messaging apps.
While this shows a clear demand for sharing music online, it also shows a gap among platforms,
as these interactions stay split across apps.
Hence, allowing this sharing directly within the streaming app could give a smoother
and more consistent experience.

Responses to the question ``Would you say that you know a lot of people with similar music taste?''
were mixed, with a near-even split.
This suggests that while some users already have a social circle with similar music taste, many do not.
In addition, when asked
``Would you like to connect with others who share your musical taste, or influence those around you to explore and appreciate the music you enjoy?'',
most answered yes.

This data further supports the idea that social discovery features could be useful in a streaming platform.
Moreover, over 60\% of participants said in Question 15 that they would use
extra social features if available on a streaming platform.


\section{Summary}
Overall, the results show that people listen to music often,
with streaming services being their main way of accessing it.
The data also show that music plays an important role in everyday life
and often appears in social situations.

Yet, existing platforms only partly support interaction between users.
This makes the idea of a music streaming service with built-in social features both relevant and
likely to meet real user needs.
